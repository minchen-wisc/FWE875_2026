Your anchor project

Everything in this course happens on one real project you choose: a thesis chapter, a planned study, or a dataset you need to analyze. Each week's work adds to a research record you keep. If you don't have a project yet, use the class project: How much of the year-to-year variation in burned area does fire-season aridity (vapor pressure deficit) explain, and does that hold across regions? It comes with three papers, public weather data, and a synthetic dataset of burned area in ten fire regions.

"Experiments" in this course are computational: no fieldwork and no wet lab. You download public data with a provenance record, check a derivation against a published worked example and an AI-written script, and analyse a dataset, your own or the class dataset, under a written plan.

Four habits, every week

Verify

Nothing an AI produces goes into your work until you have checked it against a source, a test, or your own expertise.

Log

Keep an AI-use log: tool, prompt, purpose, what you kept, and how you checked it. From Week 3 a hook writes part of it for you.

Disclose

Anything you write with AI help, in this course or in your research, ends with a specific disclosure statement. See AI rules and data.

Stay the author

AI may draft, suggest, and critique. You decide, justify, and take responsibility for every claim.

Grading

The course is graded Satisfactory/Unsatisfactory, based on attending the four sessions and taking part in the labs and exit tickets. There are no scored assignments. Between sessions you'll find suggested practices and suggested readings that carry each week's methods over to your own project; do what helps you.

Model and harness

The model only predicts text. Everything around it is the harness: the instructions it sees, the tools it can call, the loop that lets it act and check, and what it may do without asking. A connector built on the Model Context Protocol (MCP), like the Zotero connector, is one part of a harness: it gives Claude tools for an outside system. Each harness changes what you have to check. Write your project summary (no AI)

What and why

A project summary is the short description of your research you'd give a colleague: the question, the system, the data, your progress, and where you're stuck. Every prompt you write this term needs that context, and a model can only use the context you give it. Writing it yourself first makes you state what you know and don't know, and it is what later lets you judge whether Claude's answers fit your project.

  1. In Obsidian, right-click 40_Projects → New note, and name it project-summary.
  2. Write about 150 words, in plain sentences, covering: your question; the system or study area; the data you have or plan to use; what you've done so far; and what you're stuck on.
  3. No project yet? Write it about the class question, or about a question you might study.

Example (a made-up student)

I'm a first-year MS student asking whether spring drought predicts how much area burns in the pine barrens of northwest Wisconsin, where most fires happen in April and May before green-up. My study area is four counties. I plan to use state wildfire records and daily weather from Daymet, with no fieldwork. So far I've plotted fires per year and spring VPD for 2001–2024, and the wettest springs do have few fires. I'm stuck on two things: most fires are small and started by people, so I don't know whether burned area or the number of fires is the better response; and a few big years dominate every plot, so I'm not sure how to model years with almost no fire.

Why no AI here

If Claude writes your summary, every later answer will be built on its guess about your project, not on your project. A rough summary in your words beats a polished one in Claude's.

Set up the ladder

What and why

A prompt ladder is a small experiment on your own prompting: one task, run several times, each time adding one element. Because only one thing changes from rung to rung, you can see which element changed the answer. Claude in the chat app sees only what is in that chat: what you type, paste, or attach. It can't open your vault, so prompt-ladder.md is your lab notebook, not something Claude reads. You write or copy each prompt there, paste it into the chat, and bring the answer back to score it. A new chat for each rung matters for the same reason: within one chat, Claude sees everything said before, so earlier rungs would leak into later ones.

  1. Copy the worksheet (below the steps) into a new note, 00_Inbox/prompt-ladder.md. This is where you record the experiment: each prompt you send, its score, and what changed.
  2. Write at the top, in one sentence, a task you'd really ask Claude about your project, for example: "Explain to my committee why studies of fire weather use vapor pressure deficit rather than relative humidity."
  3. Open the Claude desktop app (or claude.ai). You'll use a new chat for every rung, so earlier rungs don't leak into later ones.

How each rung works

  1. Write the rung's prompt in the worksheet, under "Prompts sent".
  2. Copy it, start a new chat in the Claude app, paste it into the message box, and send.
  3. Copy the first paragraph of Claude's answer back into the worksheet and score it.

Claude never sees the worksheet or anything else in your vault. In this lab it sees only what you paste into the chat. In Lab 4 you'll use Claude Code, which runs in your vault folder and can read its notes when you ask.

Rung 0: the bare prompt

What and why

The bare prompt is your baseline. With no task, audience, or context, the model can only give the most generic answer its training suggests. Every later rung is measured against this one.

  1. New chat. Type only: Tell me about [your topic].
  2. Paste the first paragraph of the answer into the worksheet and score it 1–5 for accuracy, specificity, and usefulness.

You should see

A general overview: accurate-sounding, not about your project, often longer than you need.

Rungs 1–3: add one element at a time

What and why

Each element answers a question the model would otherwise have to guess. Task and audience say what to produce and for whom; context (your summary) says what situation it's for; constraints and format say how long, how structured, and how to flag uncertainty. The [CHECK] markers turn the answer into something you can verify piece by piece instead of something you have to trust whole.

New chat for each. Keep everything from the previous rung and add one thing:

  1. Task and audience: "Write a 200-word explanation of X for my thesis committee, who are ecologists but not atmospheric scientists."
  2. Context: open 40_Projects/project-summary.md, copy the text, and paste it inside <context> … </context> tags above the task. Claude can't open the note itself, so the summary reaches it only by pasting.
  3. Constraints and format: add a <constraints> block: length, structure, "Say when you are unsure," "Mark any claim I should verify with [CHECK]."

Score each one.

You should see

A clear jump at context or constraints. With constraints, [CHECK] markers appear next to specific claims; those are your to-verify list.

Lab 2

Citation audit: chat vs. Research mode

break-itclass tally

Goal: measure, on your own topic, how often AI-supplied references are wrong, and how the kind of error changes when Claude can search. In Lab 4 you'll see a third harness: Claude reading papers you chose.

Round 1: plain chat, no tools

What and why

In plain chat with web search off, Claude has no way to look anything up: it writes references from patterns learned in training. That makes this a clean test of how often generated references are wrong, with the model cut off from the tools that usually surround it.

  1. In the desktop app (or claude.ai), open a new chat. In the tools menu of the message box, turn web search off.
  2. Run this prompt with your topic (from your project summary):
Round 1 and 2 prompt
List 8 peer-reviewed journal articles on [your project topic]. For each, give authors, year, title, journal, volume, pages, and DOI.

You should see

Eight neat references with DOIs, and no tool steps in the conversation: nothing was looked up.

Verify every round 1 reference

What and why

A DOI resolves to one specific publication, so a DOI that opens a different paper means the citation is wrong. Crossref is the registry where publishers deposit their records, which makes it the authority for year, journal, volume, and pages. The four categories separate different failures (wrong details, invented papers, real papers that don't fit), because each needs a different check.

For each reference, in this order (stop when you've decided):

  1. DOI: paste it after https://doi.org/ in a new tab. Does it open this paper (same title and authors)?
  2. Title: search the exact title in quotes in Google Scholar. No hit is a strong warning sign.
  3. Crossref: search the title at search.crossref.org to check year, journal, volume, and pages.
  4. Topic: read the abstract. Is it about your topic?

Then choose one category and write what was wrong:

  • A, correct: exists and every detail matches.
  • B, real but wrong: exists, but year, authors, journal, pages, or DOI is wrong (a DOI that opens a different paper counts here).
  • C, fabricated: you can't find it anywhere.
  • D, off-target: real and correct, but not about your topic.

Tricky cases

Right paper, wrong year: B. Real author, plausible title, no such paper: C. A preprint cited as a journal article: B.

Round 2: Research mode

What and why

Research mode is a different harness around the same model: it runs many web searches in sequence, reads the pages it finds, and writes an answer with links to its sources. Being able to look papers up means it invents fewer of them, but it adds a new failure: the claim attached to a citation may not be in that source. That's why you open the source and search for the sentence.

  1. New chat. Turn web search on, then choose Research from the + menu in the message box.
  2. Run the same prompt. Research takes a few minutes; finish verifying round 1 while it runs.
  3. When it finishes, verify 3 of its references the same way.
  4. For one of them, click the citation marker in Claude's answer, open the source, and use Find (Ctrl/Cmd+F) to look for the claim Claude attached to it.

You should see

Many search steps, then an answer with clickable citations. Fewer invented papers. The new question: is the cited sentence really in the source?

Tally and discuss

What and why

Pooling the class's counts turns individual anecdotes into data about this model on these tasks. Comparing the two rounds shows that a better harness changes the kind of error more than it removes the need to check.

  1. Enter your counts in the shared class spreadsheet.
  2. Class discussion: which category was hardest to catch? What changed with search: the number of errors, or the kind?

Check every row you'd keep

What and why

Checking means going back to the source: finding the quote at its locator and reading the sentences around it. A row can quote real words and still misstate them, by dropping a qualifier, changing a measure, or presenting another study's finding as the paper's own. Only rows you've checked move into your notes.

What to check in every row

The measure and its scale (raw, log, mean, maximum); which variables went into it; the place, ecosystem, and period; whether a number comes from observations or from a model; whether the paper reports it or only plans it; and whether it is the paper's own finding or one it cites from another study. AI rules and data

This course follows UW–Madison's "allow AI with documentation" approach: AI use is expected on most work, and every use is logged and disclosed.

Allowed, with logging

Brainstorming, literature exploration, extracting and checking evidence, drafting and revising code, downloading public data, checking derivations, editing suggestions on prose (within your target journal's rules), debugging, explaining concepts, critiquing your own drafts, and running agents (Research mode, Cowork, Claude Code, subagents) on your own files, your vault, your Zotero library, and public data.

Not allowed

  • Submitting any citation, quotation, number, or result you have not checked against its source.
  • Adding an AI-suggested reference to Zotero as if verified. Tag it to-verify until you have opened it.
  • Generating, altering, or "filling in" research data, or making figures that misrepresent data.
  • Uploading anything in the right-hand column below, or giving an agent access to folders that hold it.
  • Letting an agent change code, files, or your Zotero library without a checkpoint you can roll back.
  • Turning on a skill, plugin, or connector whose files you have not read.
  • Running AI on a manuscript or proposal you were asked to review.
  • Writing the reflective essay with AI (spelling and grammar checks only, and say so).
  • Presenting AI output as your own idea without disclosure.

What you may upload

Claude is not one of UW–Madison's licensed AI tools, so treat your personal account as an unvetted service. UW tells users never to enter sensitive or restricted information, such as student records, health data, or unpublished research, into unvetted AI services.

When in doubt, use synthetic data with the same structure, or ask the instructor. Keep the model-training setting off (Settings → Privacy).

Agent and connector security

Connectors and skills are code

The Zotero connector is third-party software; shared skills aren't reviewed by Anthropic. Read them before you turn them on.

Watch for prompt injection

Web pages and PDFs can hide instructions aimed at AI. Read what an agent plans to do, and keep permission prompts on for actions that change files or send data.
